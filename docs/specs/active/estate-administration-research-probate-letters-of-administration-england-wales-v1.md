## 2. Research question

What do authoritative sources say about probate, grants of representation, grants of probate, and Letters of Administration in England and Wales, including who may apply, when a grant may be needed, application routes, evidence, fees, process boundaries, and uncertainty?

Subsidiary questions are:

- How do official sources distinguish an executor, administrator and personal representative?
- What changes where there is no will, or a will exists but no executor can or will act?
- Which rules are legal rules, which are HMCTS service requirements, and which are asset-holder requirements?
- What can a grant establish or enable, and what does it not settle?
- Which circumstances fall outside the ordinary public application route?
- Which claims are too fact-sensitive or high-risk for AdminAvenger to determine?

## 5. Terminology and official definitions

Official language is not completely uniform. GOV.UK often uses “probate” as a broad public label, while legislation uses it more narrowly for probate of a will. Future material must preserve the context.

## 6. Core distinction: will, executor and administrator

### Ordinary distinctions

| Situation | Ordinary grant described by official sources | Ordinary representative | Important boundary | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Will and an acting executor named in it | Grant of probate | Executor | The will and appointment still require proper evidence and may raise exception issues. | P-02, P-07 |
| No will | Letters of Administration | Administrator | The applicant must fall within the statutory priority framework; “closest relative” is only a public shorthand. | P-03, P-11 |
| Will but no executor is named or able to apply | Letters of Administration with will annexed | Administrator | Entitlement follows the Rule 20 hierarchy, not a casual choice among beneficiaries. | P-07, P-08, P-11, P-13 |

### Why the will does not answer every application question

- A will may name several executors, a substitute executor, or no executor. [P-02]
- An executor may hold power reserved, renounce, appoint an attorney, have died, be under 18, or lack capacity. [P-02, P-10, P-11]
- The original will may be lost, damaged, altered or accompanied by codicils. [P-02, P-10, P-35]
- A document’s apparent title does not establish validity, due execution, revocation or legal effect.
- Where no executor proves the will, Rule 20 contains a detailed priority order for administration with will annexed. [P-11]
- A grant type is an HMCTS outcome, not a conclusion AdminAvenger may make from document fragments.

Safe research summary: a named, acting executor usually seeks a grant of probate; an entitled applicant usually seeks Letters of Administration where there is no will; an entitled non-executor may seek Letters of Administration with will annexed where a will exists but no executor receives the grant. Every part remains conditional on the facts, evidence, rules and court process.

## 7. When a grant may be needed

### Clearly established

- GOV.UK says to contact each financial organisation used by the person who died because every organisation has its own rules. [P-01]
- GOV.UK says a grant may not be needed where the person only had savings, held shares or money jointly that pass to surviving owners, or owned land as beneficial joint tenants that passes to surviving owners. The wording is conditional, not categorical. [P-01]
- HM Land Registry requires grant evidence for ordinary dealings with a sole owner’s registered property, while the death of one joint proprietor can follow a different route. [P-21, P-22]
- A bank, building society, insurer, investment platform or other holder may decide what evidence it requires to release or transfer an asset. Its operational threshold is not a universal probate threshold. [P-01]

### Institution-dependent

- No researched official source establishes one universal estate-value threshold at which a grant is always required.
- An institution may release a lower-value asset without a grant, but that does not decide whether another asset-holder or property transaction requires one.
- An institution’s request for a grant is evidence of that institution’s requirement, not a determination for the whole estate.
- The £5,000 figure in the HMCTS fee order decides whether the court application fee is payable. It is not a universal “probate threshold”. [P-05, P-15]

### Jointly owned assets

- Beneficial joint ownership may cause an asset to pass by survivorship, but ownership form must be checked rather than inferred from two names. [P-01, P-21]
- Tenants in common and sole ownership have different consequences. HM Land Registry guidance recommends legal help where the position is unclear or complex. [P-21, P-22]
- The fact that one asset passes outside the estate does not establish that no grant is needed for other assets.

### What cannot be known from a document alone

AdminAvenger must not conclude:

- that a grant is or is not required for the estate;
- that an account balance is below an institution’s release limit applicable at the time;
- that joint ownership carries survivorship;
- that every asset has been identified;
- that an organisation will accept an indemnity, death certificate or certified copy instead of a grant;
- that the probate fee threshold is an asset-holder threshold;
- that a small or apparently simple estate has no tax, debt, ownership or dispute issue.

## 8. Who may apply

### Where there is a will

- GOV.UK says an executor named in the will or codicil can apply. [P-02]
- If several executors are named, they must agree who applies; up to four may be named on the application. [P-02] Senior Courts Act 1981 section 114 separately prohibits a grant to more than four persons for the same part of an estate. [P-13]
- An executor not applying may reserve power, provided the relevant notice requirements are handled. Power reserved retains the possibility of a later application and is not renunciation. [P-02, P-11]
- PA15 was the HMCTS form page identified at the recorded access date for permanent renunciation by an executor and, according to its updated landing page, some administrators. It must not be selected automatically without checking the guidance applicable at the time and the circumstances. [P-10]
- PA11 is the support form by which an executor may appoint an attorney for the grant in the circumstances described by GOV.UK. [P-02, P-10]
- If an executor has died, lacks capacity or cannot act, the substitute terms in the will and Rules 32 and 35 may matter. This is an exception path, not a simple beneficiary substitution. [P-02, P-11]

### Where there is no will

Rule 22 of the Non-Contentious Probate Rules 1987 places persons with a beneficial interest into this priority order for a wholly intestate estate:

1. surviving spouse or civil partner;
2. children and issue of a child who died before the deceased;
3. parents;
4. whole-blood siblings and issue of a whole-blood sibling who died before the deceased;
5. half-blood siblings and issue of a half-blood sibling who died before the deceased;
6. grandparents;
7. whole-blood uncles and aunts and issue of one who died before the deceased;
8. half-blood uncles and aunts and issue of one who died before the deceased.

The Rule also covers Crown claims, creditors, personal representatives and other exceptions. It must be read in full before any legal conclusion. [P-11]

GOV.UK provides a simplified ordinary statement: the most entitled person can apply, normally the spouse or civil partner followed by adult children; an unmarried partner cannot apply merely because they were the partner. [P-03] This does not replace Rule 22.

### Will but no acting executor

Rule 20 begins with the executor, then establishes a detailed hierarchy involving residuary beneficiaries, their personal representatives, other beneficiaries and creditors. [P-11] The PA1P guidance says a beneficiary may be able to use the form where there are no executors able to apply or none named. [P-08] Neither source supports telling an arbitrary beneficiary that they are entitled.

### Multiple people in the same degree

- A grant can ordinarily be made without notice to others in the same degree, subject to the Rules. [P-11]
- A dispute between people in the same degree goes before a district judge or registrar. Rule 27(6A), inserted in 2025, permits an order considering majority support, first lodged application, an agreed neutral person, or a registry-appointed neutral person. [P-11, P-12]
- This hierarchy is a court mechanism, not a product recommendation or scoring rule.

### Applicants under 18 and life interests

- GOV.UK’s ordinary no-will guidance describes children applying at 18 or over. [P-03]
- Rule 32 provides for a limited administration for a minor’s use and benefit through specified adults or entities. [P-11]
- Senior Courts Act 1981 section 114 ordinarily requires a trust corporation or at least two individuals for a grant of administration where a beneficiary is a minor or a life interest arises, unless the court considers a sole administrator expedient. [P-13]
- AdminAvenger must not decide who should act for a minor.

### Applicants lacking capacity

Rule 35 contains an ordered mechanism involving a Court of Protection-authorised person, a lawful attorney under a registered EPA or LPA, a residuary beneficiary, and court directions. [P-11] GOV.UK also points to PA14 for medical evidence in the described executor situation. [P-02, P-10] This requires careful case-specific handling and likely professional help.

### Professional representatives and trust corporations

- A person may instruct a solicitor or probate practitioner; GOV.UK distinguishes professional charges from court fees. [P-06, P-27]
- “Probate practitioner” is a regulated statutory label in the Rules. [P-11]
- Trust-corporation applications must use the online portal under the Rules as amended in 2025. [P-11, P-12]
- AdminAvenger must not assume a firm is authorised merely because it offers estate services. Check the relevant regulator. [P-33]

## 9. Preconditions before applying

The ordinary sequence supported by the cited sources at their recorded access dates is:

1. Register the death or obtain the applicable coroner documentation. Cross-reference the death-registration dossier; this document does not re-research that process.
2. Check whether any grant is needed and whether the proposed applicant appears to fall within the relevant official route. [P-01]
3. Locate the latest original will and all codicils where a will exists. Do not alter, staple, unfasten or mark them. [P-02, P-08]
4. Estimate the estate values needed for inheritance-tax and probate reporting. [P-04]
5. Determine through HMRC guidance applicable when the application is made whether the estate is excepted or full details must be reported. [P-04, P-20]
6. If required, send IHT400 to HMRC and start paying any IHT that must be paid before the grant. [P-04, P-19]
7. Wait for HMRC’s unique probate code where the full account route applies. Current practitioner guidance says to allow 20 working days and contact HMRC if the code has not arrived. [P-18, P-20]
8. Select the application route then available and verify the route-specific checklist immediately before submission. [P-06, P-08, P-09, P-17]

Important separations:

- Death registration supplies evidence of death; it does not confer estate authority.
- Estate valuation and IHT reporting are linked preconditions but are separate tax processes.
- An excepted-estate declaration does not establish that no tax issue can ever arise.
- HMRC’s probate code does not grant probate.
- Starting an application does not authorise dealing with assets.
- The death certificate requirement differs by route. The general online guidance accessed for this research asks for a copy for a death outside England or Wales or an interim coroner certificate; the citizen paper forms accessed require an official death or interim certificate. [P-04, P-08, P-09] The route-specific checklist applicable at submission controls.

No inheritance-tax calculation is performed or authorised here.

### Route differences that must remain visible

- A grant of probate, Letters of Administration, and Letters of Administration with will annexed are distinct case types. [P-07, P-18]
- Practitioner online eligibility for Letters of Administration without a will is narrower than the full legal class of people who may be entitled; the guidance accessed for this research lists conditions such as a sole applicant and ordinary spouse/civil-partner or sole-child cases. [P-17]
- Practitioner online Letters of Administration with will annexed is limited to specified sole-applicant/residuary-beneficiary circumstances. [P-17]
- Lost originals, copy or reconstructed wills, foreign wills, domicile questions, attorney applications, same-degree disputes, rectification and discretionary grants can leave the ordinary route. [P-17, P-18, P-35]
- Online eligibility is operational and mutable. It is not a statement of substantive legal entitlement.

Current service names:

- Personal applications: GOV.UK “Applying for probate” and the Apply for Probate service. [P-06]
- Professional applications: MyHMCTS. [P-18]
- Postal forms: PA1P or PA1A from the HMCTS probate forms collection. [P-10]

## 13. Submission and processing

### Submission

- Personal online applications are submitted through the Apply for Probate service. [P-06]
- Citizen paper applications and evidence go to the address on the PA1P or PA1A instructions applicable at submission. Addresses must be rechecked at use. [P-08, P-09]
- Practitioner applications ordinarily use MyHMCTS, with paper used for listed exceptions. [P-17, P-18]
- Original documents required for an online case are sent separately in accordance with registry instructions. [P-11, P-18]

### Review and contact

- The Probate Service reviews an application. [P-07]
- Online personal applicants can track through the probate service; practitioner MyHMCTS statuses include awaiting documents, case stopped, ready to issue, grant issued and post-grant review. [P-07, P-18]
- For a paper application, GOV.UK says the applicant should receive a letter or email within 12 weeks and can contact the service if they do not. [P-07]
- A stopped case means HMCTS needs an error, evidence issue or external dependency resolved. It is not a refusal or proof of wrongdoing. [P-18, P-31]
- HMCTS may contact an applicant for further information. AdminAvenger may help organise the request and draft questions, but not answer a statement of truth or make a legal admission.

### Original will handling

When a grant is approved, HMCTS keeps the will and codicils and they become public records. The general guidance accessed for this research says a death certificate sent in is returned. [P-07, P-08]

### Processing information

- The public guide says a grant is usually received within 12 weeks and may take longer where more information is required. [P-06, P-07]
- Official January-to-March 2026 statistics reported different observed averages: five weeks overall for probate grants, approximately two weeks for digital non-stopped probate grants, and 14 weeks for stopped probate grants. Letters of Administration averages differed again. [P-31]
- These measures use different populations and periods. None is a legal deadline or guarantee.
- “Usually”, “average”, “service estimate” and “statutory deadline” must never be merged.

### Approval and correction

- If approved, HMCTS issues a grant of probate, Letters of Administration with will annexed, or Letters of Administration according to the case. [P-07]
- GOV.UK tells a recipient to return an incorrect grant to the district probate registry named on it. [P-07]
- Senior Courts Act 1981 section 121 allows the High Court to call in and revoke a grant that should not have been made or contains an error. That is a court power, not a self-service correction rule. [P-13]
- The ordinary sources do not establish a complete public withdrawal, amendment or revocation workflow. Obtain HMCTS information or legal advice for a submitted or issued case.

## 14. The grant and what it enables

### What official sources support

- GOV.UK describes the issued document as allowing the recipient to start dealing with the estate and tells them to send copies to asset-holding organisations. [P-07]
- HM Land Registry requires grant evidence for ordinary transfers or sales involving a deceased sole owner’s registered property. [P-21, P-22]
- Administration of Estates Act 1925 section 25 places a duty on the personal representative to collect, get in and administer the estate according to law. [P-14]
- Sealed or official copies allow the grant to be shown to more than one organisation. [P-05, P-07]

### What the grant does not establish by itself

The grant does not by itself prove:

- that every asset or debt has been identified;
- that the will is immune from challenge or has been interpreted correctly;
- that tax has been fully and finally settled;
- that the estate is solvent;
- that a debt is valid or has the claimed priority;
- that an asset belonged beneficially to the deceased;
- that a person is entitled to a particular inheritance or share;
- that distribution is lawful or complete;
- that an institution must accept every act proposed by the representative;
- that no caveat, claim, correction or later court order can affect the position.

HMRC’s IHT400 notes explicitly warn that receiving the grant does not mean all IHT and interest have been paid. [P-19]

### Research caution

The public phrase “legal right” is service shorthand. The underlying effect can differ between executors and administrators and may depend on the act proposed. Future corpus wording should prefer: “The grant is the official document organisations commonly ask to see before allowing the personal representative to deal with estate assets.” Any stronger legal proposition needs specialist review.

## 16. Caveats and disputes boundary

### High-level verified facts

- A caveat is entered by a person wishing to show cause against sealing a grant. [P-11]
- GOV.UK says it can be used where there is a dispute about who may apply, whether a will exists, or whether it is legal. [P-23]
- An effective caveat ordinarily prevents sealing of a grant for six months and can be extended under the Rules. [P-11, P-23]
- Entering a caveat can lead to legal proceedings and costs. [P-23]
- The statutory entry or extension fee was £4 as at the research date. [P-15]

### Prohibited product behaviour

AdminAvenger must not advise whether to:

- enter a caveat;
- threaten a caveat;
- warn a caveator;
- enter an appearance;
- issue a summons;
- renew, withdraw, challenge or remove a caveat;
- start a probate claim;
- accept a compromise;
- concede validity, capacity, fraud, undue influence or entitlement.

A dispute about a will, representative or grant can become time-sensitive and expensive. The safe response is to preserve documents, avoid asserting legal conclusions, identify the official process at a high level, and suggest prompt advice from a solicitor or authorised probate practitioner.

No contentious-probate workflow is authorised.

## 17. Privacy and sensitive information

Probate work commonly involves:

- death certificates and coroner documents;
- original wills and codicils;
- home and correspondence addresses;
- dates of birth and death;
- National Insurance numbers;
- inheritance-tax references and unique probate codes;
- bank, investment, pension and insurance details;
- property and mortgage details;
- asset values, debts and funeral costs;
- family relationships and adoption information;
- beneficiary information;
- signatures and witness details;
- identity and medical-capacity documents;
- email addresses and mobile numbers for joint applicants;
- probate application and case references.

Official sources also establish that:

- the original will and codicils become public records after grant; [P-02, P-07, P-08]
- online services require personal information and have their own privacy notices; [P-29]
- HMCTS may communicate by email, letter, telephone, webchat or text and may retain contact records; [P-29]
- paper probate PDFs may not be suitable for all assistive technology, and HMCTS provides an alternative-format request route; [P-08, P-09, P-30]
- the personal online service has digital support for people without internet access or confidence. [P-06, P-32]

Future product implications:

- collect only the minimum needed for the immediate user-directed task;
- do not ask for a will, death certificate, tax reference, full account number or family tree merely to explain a general process;
- keep processing local-first;
- warn before a user imports or shares sensitive documents;
- encourage redaction where full detail is unnecessary;
- never infer authority, priority, parentage, marriage, adoption or beneficiary status from names alone;
- treat signatures, probate codes and case references as sensitive;
- explain that an original will sent to HMCTS may become public after grant;
- never place real estate information or personal relationships in examples, fixtures or evaluation material;
- do not silently transmit, submit or retain any application information.

No storage schema is proposed.

### Accessibility findings

- At its recorded access date, the PA1P page offered English, Welsh and large-print citizen versions. [P-08]
- PA1A is available in English and Welsh; its page warns that the PDF may not suit all assistive technology and provides an alternative-format request route. [P-09]
- HMCTS policy says users can request an alternative format where a published document is inaccessible. [P-30]
- The personal probate guide provides a digital-support route for people who lack internet access or confidence using the service. [P-06, P-32]
- A paper route exists, but it may be slower and has its own evidence and payment requirements. Accessibility must not be reduced to “use paper”. [P-06, P-08, P-09]

No independent accessibility audit of the live personal or practitioner service was conducted. Future research should test keyboard navigation, screen-reader output, zoom, error recovery, Welsh-language parity, cognitive load, save-and-return behaviour, and the accessibility of evidence-upload or postal instructions.

### Emotional-safety implications requiring approval

The official process contains legal declarations, original documents, sensitive family facts, variable waiting times and potentially permanent choices. As product-safety judgements, future wording should:

- avoid blame, shame and unnecessary urgency;
- explain one bounded step at a time;
- make it easy to pause and resume;
- distinguish a correctable stopped application from a refusal;
- avoid implying that delay reflects personal failure;
- warn clearly before permanent or dispute-related steps;
- preserve genuine deadlines without turning estimates into countdowns;
- offer preparation and signposting while leaving every consequential decision to the user.

## 18. Source-grounding rules for future use

### Claim classes

| Class | What may be said | Evidence required | Example boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Document-observed fact | Describe text visibly present in a user-provided document | Exact source span and document identity | “The letter says the organisation requires a grant.” Not: “A grant is legally required for the estate.” |
| Time-sensitive official process | Explain a government or court step as at a stated date | Tier 1 or Tier 2 source checked at the point of use, jurisdiction and access or review date | “The PA1P page checked on [date] says to send the original will.” |
| Conditional official guidance | Use qualifiers and preserve conditions | Official source plus the condition | “Some estates may not need a grant; each institution has its own rules.” |
| Institution-specific requirement | Attribute the requirement to that organisation | Organisation document or communication applicable to the case | “This bank’s letter asks for…” |
| Legal rule | State only a reviewed proposition within its scope | Applicable legislation version plus specialist review for product use | “Rule 22 sets an order of priority.” Do not select the winning applicant. |
| Product inference | Label as an inference or suggested question | Source facts plus human approval | “It may help to ask whether the account is jointly held.” |
| Prohibited conclusion | Do not state | No ordinary evidence is enough for product determination | Will validity, grant entitlement, tax liability, solvency, distribution legality. |

### Source-supported facts

A future system may report, if the underlying source is visible:

- a document names a person as executor;
- a will or codicil appears to name a person;
- a letter asks for an original will or grant;
- a probate reference appears;
- a fee or deadline is stated in the submitted text;
- an institution says it requires a grant;
- an HMCTS status says the case is stopped or awaiting documents.

It must preserve “appears”, “states”, “asks” or equivalent attribution where legal effect has not been established.

### General official guidance

With an England and Wales source checked at the point of use, a future response may explain:

- a grant of probate ordinarily relates to an executor named in a will;
- Letters of Administration ordinarily relate to an administrator;
- Letters of Administration with will annexed may apply where a will exists but no executor receives the grant;
- institutions can set their own evidence requirements;
- some estates may not need a grant;
- a £5,000 court-fee threshold is not a universal probate threshold;
- a grant starts, rather than completes, the asset-administration phase.

### Must remain uncertain

- validity, latest status or interpretation of a will or codicil;
- legal entitlement to apply;
- whether a grant is required for all or any particular estate assets;
- completeness of asset and debt identification;
- correctness or completeness of tax treatment;
- whether a family relationship creates priority;
- validity or effect of renunciation, attorney authority or power reserved;
- existence or merits of a dispute;
- estate solvency;
- lawful distribution or beneficiary shares.

### High-risk claims never to make without authoritative evidence and human review

- probate is definitely required or unnecessary;
- a person has legal authority;
- a person is the highest-priority applicant;
- a will or codicil is valid, invalid, revoked or controlling;
- an executor can, cannot or must act;
- an administrator is entitled to the grant;
- a renunciation, attorney appointment or caveat is valid;
- tax is payable, fully paid or correctly reported;
- an inheritance or intestacy share is due;
- a debt is valid, enforceable, secured, priority or payable;
- the estate is solvent or insolvent;
- an asset belongs to the estate or passes by survivorship;
- a statutory deadline applies or has expired;
- an HMCTS estimate is a guaranteed completion date;
- a grant proves administration is complete;
- professional representation is legally required or unnecessary.

## 20. Decision boundaries for a future system

### A future system must not determine

- legal entitlement to a grant;
- validity, interpretation or revocation of a will or codicil;
- whether a grant is definitely required;
- statutory applicant priority from incomplete facts;
- whether a family relationship is legally established;
- tax liability or tax-reporting correctness;
- estate solvency;
- validity, enforceability or priority of a debt;
- beneficiary entitlement or distribution shares;
- ownership of a disputed or jointly held asset;
- domicile;
- validity or effect of renunciation, power reserved, attorney authority or caveat;
- outcome or merits of a probate dispute;
- whether a solicitor or practitioner must be instructed;
- whether an applicant should sign a statement of truth.

### A future system may prepare

- plain-English explanations of verified official terminology;
- attribution of what a user’s document appears to request;
- source excerpts within permitted quotation limits;
- evidence and document inventories;
- unanswered-question lists;
- route-neutral preparation checklists for human review;
- links to official services and forms checked at the point of use;
- visible uncertainty and jurisdiction labels;
- questions for HMCTS, HMRC, an asset-holder or legal adviser;
- draft notes that the user decides whether to save, send or use.

It must never submit, sign, pay, notify, appoint, renounce, reserve power, enter a caveat, or contact an organisation without explicit user action and confirmation.

## 23. Verified findings

### Clearly established

- “Grant of representation” covers probate and administration; the principal ordinary outputs are grant of probate, Letters of Administration and Letters of Administration with will annexed. [P-07, P-14]
- Executor, administrator and personal representative are distinct but related roles. [P-02, P-03, P-14]
- Rule 20 governs priority where a will exists; Rule 22 governs ordinary wholly intestate priority. [P-11]
- No grant can be made to more than four persons for the same part of an estate. [P-13]
- Before applying, applicants generally address valuation and the applicable IHT reporting sequence. [P-04, P-19, P-20]
- Personal online and paper routes exist; practitioners ordinarily use MyHMCTS with defined paper exceptions. [P-06, P-17, P-18]
- Original wills are retained and become public records after grant. [P-02, P-07, P-08]
- A grant enables the representative to begin dealing with assets and is commonly presented to asset-holders. [P-07, P-21]

### Conditional

- An executor named in a will ordinarily applies for probate, if able and willing and no exception changes the route. [P-02]
- An entitled relative ordinarily applies for Letters of Administration where no will exists, subject to Rule 22 and case facts. [P-03, P-11]
- A beneficiary may apply for Letters of Administration with will annexed only where the official priority and route permit it. [P-08, P-11]
- A grant may not be needed for some jointly held assets or lower-value holdings, but the ownership and institution rules must be checked. [P-01, P-21]

### Institution-dependent

- Banks and other organisations decide what evidence they require to release assets. [P-01]
- An organisation’s internal release threshold is not a legal universal.
- Certified or sealed copies and additional evidence may be requested according to the organisation and transaction. [P-05, P-07]

### Process-dependent

- Online eligibility, evidence, addresses, payment method and contact routes depend on the service available at the time and the case type. [P-06, P-08, P-09, P-17, P-18]
- At the recorded access dates, HMRC guidance applied the unique probate code to the documented full-account sequence. [P-04, P-19, P-20]
- Processing time depends strongly on grant type, route, document receipt and whether HMCTS stops the case. [P-07, P-18, P-31]

### Unresolved

- A complete, user-facing map from every Rule 20 category to the personal or practitioner application routes documented at the access dates has not been established.
- The public guidance accessed for this research does not fully explain withdrawal or amendment of a submitted personal application.
- The boundary between procedural HMCTS help and legal advice is clear in principle but not exhaustively described for every enquiry type.
- Public-facing language does not consistently explain the different legal effect of probate for an executor and administration for an administrator.
- Accessibility of the live personal online application was not independently audited in this research.

## 26. Product implications

These are research implications, not approved designs.

### Language to avoid

- “You do/don’t need probate.”
- “You are the executor/administrator.”
- “You are next in line.”
- “This will is valid.”
- “The estate is small, so no grant is needed.”
- “Probate costs £526” without date, conditions and fee-source link.
- “Probate takes 12 weeks.”
- “The grant means the estate is finished.”
- “The bank must release the money.”
- “You should renounce / reserve power / enter a caveat.”

### Uncertainty that must stay visible

- jurisdiction;
- grant type;
- source date;
- will and executor status;
- applicant-priority uncertainty;
- asset ownership;
- institution-specific requirements;
- tax and debt separation;
- form and route eligibility;
- dispute and complexity indicators.

### Facts requiring source evidence

- every fee, form, address, contact detail or time estimate;
- a stated executor, beneficiary or administrator;
- a will or codicil date;
- an organisation’s grant requirement or threshold;
- an application reference, status or HMCTS request;
- any deadline;
- any statement that a grant has issued.

### Escalation indicators

- no original will, alterations or competing wills;
- no acting executor;
- minor or capacity issue;
- same-degree competing applicants;
- caveat, threatened claim or family dispute;
- foreign, trust, domicile or prior-grant element;
- potentially insolvent estate;
- disputed ownership, debt or beneficiary;
- urgent sale or legal correspondence.

### Why a yes/no “Do I need probate?” answer is unsafe

The answer can depend on asset ownership, the institution holding each asset, property transactions, the will, who can act and facts not present in a document. The court-fee threshold does not answer the question. A safer future interaction would organise asset-holder questions and preserve uncertainty for the human.

### Why asset-holder requirements must remain separate

A bank’s rule establishes what that bank asks for. It does not determine HM Land Registry requirements, another institution’s policy, applicant entitlement or the law for the whole estate.

### Why representative distinctions need care

Executor, administrator and personal representative overlap but are not interchangeable. A future system should preserve the exact title used by the source and avoid inferring it from relationship or possession.

### Why grant does not mean completion

The grant enables estate administration to proceed. Tax review, collecting assets, paying valid liabilities, resolving ownership and distributing the estate remain separate. [P-14, P-19]
